Today's distinctive lesson Performance claims require credible experimental design: a question, a baseline, controlled variables, representative workloads, repetitions, honest statistics, and explicit threats to validity.

00:00

Define the five words (say them slowly)

  • Measurement: a number from an instrument ("this call took 2.4 ms").
  • Benchmark: a designed procedure producing measurements under stated conditions.
  • Evidence: measurements that bear on a specific question, with their uncertainty.
  • Interpretation: an explanation of the evidence within the conditions tested.
  • Conclusion: what we are entitled to claim — usually narrower than we want.

00:15

Say

"Write the decision rule before you see the data. Otherwise every result can be rationalised into the answer you wanted."

Falsifiable vs not

"Our design performs well" cannot fail. "The hybrid's p99 is within 20% of the trie's at 2M suggestions" can. Only the second is a hypothesis.

Baseline quality

A good baseline is simple, correct and reasonable: the standard library, the obvious design, or the current production system. Beating a deliberately weak baseline (a debug build, an O(n²) straw man) proves nothing. Reviewers check baselines first.

Confounders: what else changed?

A confounder is anything that differs between conditions besides the variable you are studying.

  • Different input data or ordering
  • Compiler, flags, library, allocator versions
  • Warm vs cold caches; first run pays setup
  • Background load; CPU frequency (turbo, thermal throttling, power saving)
  • Different machines or dates
  • Measurement order (A always first)
Controls
  • Same binary flags, same input files, same seeds
  • Warm-up runs, then interleave A and B (ABAB…)
  • Quiet machine; record CPU governor / power mode
  • Script everything; record the environment automatically

00:22

Interleaving

Running all A repetitions and then all B repetitions lets slow drifts (thermal throttling, background tasks starting) bias one design. Interleaving or randomising the order spreads drift over both. Cheap and rarely done.

Laptops

On battery, CPUs scale frequency aggressively; plugged in and "performance mode" gives more stable numbers. Report which. Turbo boost makes the first seconds faster than the rest — longer runs or disabling turbo reduce the effect.

Workloads and scale

Representative vs synthetic

  • Traces (real requests): realistic, may be unavailable or private
  • Synthetic generators: controllable, reproducible; must be justified (distribution, skew, sizes)
  • Use both where possible: synthetic to explore, trace to confirm

Scale selection

  • Sweep sizes across cache boundaries (L2, LLC, beyond)
  • Include the production size, or explain why you cannot
  • Include adversarial and skewed cases, not only the average
Course examples where the workload reversed the conclusion: strided keys (L2), scans vs shifting popularity (L3), prefix lengths (L4), update positions (L6), hot keys (L11).

00:28

Say

"Look at the warning box. Five times this semester, changing the workload — not the code — reversed the answer. The workload generator is not a detail of your benchmark; it is half of your argument."

Input distributions

Uniform random keys are the most common and least realistic choice. Real access patterns are skewed (Zipf), bursty, and correlated in time. If you use uniform, justify it or add a skewed variant.

Repetitions and summaries

  • Timing is a distribution: repeat (≥ 10–30)
  • Median: robust to outliers; report it by default
  • Percentiles (p90, p99): what slow users feel
  • Mean: sensitive to outliers; needed for throughput/cost sums
  • Spread: min–max, IQR, or a confidence interval
sort 200k  n=30
  median = 10.54 ms  [95% CI 10.51–10.59]
  p10 = 10.40   p90 = 10.84
  min = 10.34   max = 10.98   mean = 10.58

# bench_harness.hpp: summarize(samples)
# CI = bootstrap of the median (intuitive,
#      not a formal test)
If two designs' intervals overlap heavily, you do not have evidence of a difference — collect more data or accept "no measurable difference".
A confidence interval describes uncertainty in the observed measurements under the sampling assumptions. It does not remove systematic bias from unequal workloads, compiler differences, thermal effects, poor design or an unrepresentative workload.

00:35

Confidence intervals, intuitively

"If we repeated the whole experiment many times, intervals built this way would contain the true median about 95% of the time." The bootstrap in bench_harness.hpp resamples your own measurements to estimate how much the median would move. No formula to memorise; the habit is: never report one number without its spread.

What a CI cannot do (say this explicitly)

A narrow interval only says the random variation of this measurement procedure is small. If A was built with -O0 and B with -O2, or B got sorted input, you get a very precise estimate of a meaningless difference. Bootstrap also assumes the runs are roughly independent draws from the same conditions; thermal throttling or a background job drifting over time violates that. Design (Part 2) removes bias; statistics only describe noise.

Tail latency

For services, p99 matters more than the mean: a request that fans out to 100 backends waits for the slowest, so each backend's p99 becomes the user's median. Report tails; they are where contention, GC, rehash pauses (L2) and cache misses (L3) show up.

Outliers

Do not delete outliers silently. Report them, explain them (OS interrupt? first-run effect?), and use robust summaries.

00:42

Amortisation

A structure that is cheap per query but expensive to build must be judged over its lifetime: build cost ÷ number of queries before the next rebuild. The L5 index costs 720 ms to build at 50k documents; if the data is rebuilt every minute and queried 10 times, the scan wins. If it is queried millions of times, the index wins. The ratio is the argument.

00:50 Run ./pit live (~2 s), then the -O0 build.

P1 explained

The compiler proves the sum is never used and deletes the loop. A benchmark that "discovers" 0 ns has measured nothing. Defence: consume results (do_not_optimize in the harness, Google Benchmark's DoNotOptimize), and sanity-check that time scales with n.

The opposite trap

Compilers can also hoist work out of a timed loop (compute once, reuse) if inputs do not change between iterations. Vary inputs or make them opaque.

Timer resolution

For operations below ~1 µs, time a batch and divide (the L5 note about rare-term queries near 1 µs). steady_clock, not system_clock (which can jump).

Benchmark vs profile

Benchmark

How fast / expensive is the system under workload W?

Output: numbers to compare designs or versions.

Profile

Where is the time / memory being spent?

Output: a breakdown by function, line, allocation site.

measureprofilechange one thingre-measure
  • CPU profilers: sampling (perf, VTune, Visual Studio, gprof's sampling part) — low overhead; instrumentation — exact counts, higher overhead
  • Memory profilers: heaptrack, Massif, allocation counters (our counting allocator)
  • Flame graphs: stacks as widths — the widest tower is where time goes

00:58

Measurement overhead

Profilers perturb what they measure: instrumentation adds work to every call (small functions look expensive), and sampling misses short or rare events. Profile to find where; benchmark without the profiler to measure how much.

Flame graph in one sentence

Stack samples are merged; each box is a function, its width is the share of samples containing it, and its children sit on top. Look for wide plateaus near the top: code that is expensive by itself.

Tools on this course's machines

Windows: Visual Studio profiler, VTune, Very Sleepy; MinGW ships gprof. Linux: perf record -g + flame graph scripts, heaptrack, valgrind --tool=massif. The manual phase timers in profile_me.cpp work everywhere.

Activity: guess the bottleneck (15 min)

profile_me.cpp: parse 300,000 access-log lines, count per endpoint, sort the endpoints.

  1. Guess (write it down): which phase dominates, and its share? — generating input · parsing · hash-map updates · sorting
  2. Benchmark: ./prof
  3. Profile: -DPHASE_TIMERS (or gprof / perf)
  4. Propose one change; what will you re-measure?

Collect guesses first

Show of hands per phase. Most students pick sorting ("sorting is O(n log n)") or the hash map.

Actual (instructor machine)

Parsing with std::regex: 672 of 679 ms (99%). Sorting 500 endpoints: 0.0 ms — n is 500, not 300,000. Input generation (136 ms) is outside the timed region.

Fix and re-measure

Instructor_Only/Lecture_12/profile_me_fixed.cpp: a hand-written parser over std::string_view and heterogeneous hash lookup (no temporary string per line): 25.6 ms, identical output (~26×). Then the profile changes: input generation now dominates the whole program. "Optimisation moves the bottleneck; re-profile after each change."

Teaching points

  • Intuition about asymptotic complexity is a poor predictor of where time goes: the O(n log n) step works on n = 500.
  • Library choices (regex engines vary 10–100× across implementations) are performance decisions.
  • Verify the optimised output equals the original — a faster wrong program is not an optimisation.

Reproducibility checklist

  • Scripts: one command regenerates every figure from raw data
  • Raw data committed (CSV), not only plots
  • Environment recorded: CPU, cores, OS, compiler + version + flags, allocator, commit hash, seeds, date
  • Inputs: versioned files or seeded generators
  • Visualisation: axes labelled with units; log scales stated; error bars or distributions, not bare bars
  • Plot the same metric for all designs on one chart
  • Show where the curves cross, not only one size

01:20

Say

"Documentation/reproducibility is 2 marks of your project, and it is what lets me trust your 4 benchmarking marks. If I cannot rerun your benchmark from the README, I cannot check your claims."

Chart advice

For scaling experiments, plot throughput vs threads (L11) or latency vs size on a log axis (L4, L5), one line per design, with a band or error bars for the spread. A bar chart of two means with no spread is the weakest possible figure.

Part 5 · 02:05–02:20

Validity and honest reporting

Threats to validity

TypeQuestionExample threat
InternalDid the design cause the difference?Different flags, inputs or machines (confounders)
ExternalDoes the result transfer?Synthetic workload, one CPU, small n; shared_mutex 60–400× slower on MinGW only
ConstructDoes the metric measure what we care about?Mean latency for a user-facing p99 SLO; hit rate for a latency goal
StatisticalIs the difference larger than the noise?One run; overlapping intervals
Correlation is not causation: "the new version is faster since we changed the map" — did anything else change in that commit?

02:05

Performance portability

Results depend on hardware (cache sizes, cores, prefetchers), OS (scheduler, mutex implementation), compiler, standard library and allocator. The strongest external-validity evidence is the same experiment on a second, different machine. At minimum, state the conditions.

Causation in practice

A "before/after" comparison across a commit that changed five things cannot attribute the gain to one of them. Change one thing at a time, or measure each change separately.

Cherry-picking and negative results

Cherry-picking
  • Reporting the best run, the best size, the best workload
  • Dropping configurations where the new design loses
  • Stopping the experiment when the result looks good
Negative results are results
  • "The trie did not justify its memory at our scale" is a valid engineering outcome
  • Report where your design loses — it defines when to choose another
  • In this course: full marks are possible for a well-measured negative result

What to look for

  • Claims that cannot fail.
  • A single workload (especially uniform keys only).
  • Missing environment information and raw data.
  • "Faster" without a percentile and a spread.
  • No statement of what the experiment does not show.
